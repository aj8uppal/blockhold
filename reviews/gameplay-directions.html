What exists today

The foundation is already substantial.

Blockhold has eight tower families, Mythics and Seraph fusion; campaign and Frontier maps; boss hunts; daily battles; the Three Watches; and an editable, shareable Hold. Adding another menu or another damage tier is unlikely to address every source of repetition.

During a battle

The current endless generator draws groups from enemy pools, varies lanes and timing, and inserts bosses. It offers variety, but does not explicitly author every wave around a particular tactical question.

Wave generation in the current code ↗

At the endgame

Support mechanics already exist: wards, healing, summons, silence, vulnerability, grounding and crowd control. Better encounters could make those interactions easier to notice and more valuable.

Existing Mythic powers ↗

Between battles

Your Hold already displays earned buildings, trophies and banners, supports customization, and shares visit links. Its next opportunity is helping a player choose a satisfying goal.

Existing rewards and requirements ↗

These are code observations, not a measured retention diagnosis. Your reports suggest repetition and late-game tower dominance; fresh playtests need to distinguish balance problems from a lack of decisions.

What we can borrow from other tower defense games

Ninja Kiwi presents boss events, linked-map Odysseys, quests, cosmetic trophies and player-created challenges as distinct parts of Bloons TD 6. Each offers a different reason to return. Ninja Kiwi’s official game page ↗

My design inference: Blockhold should vary the question a battle asks before greatly increasing the number of systems a player must learn. This comparison is inspiration, not evidence that copying a feature will improve retention here.

Compare the directions

Five different kinds of replay value.

Each option below can stand on its own. The effort ranges are preliminary estimates for one developer covering implementation, mobile/co-op checks and initial tuning; they are not delivery commitments. Unknowns grow sharply for expeditions.

A · Recommended first

Encounters with a point

1–2 weekssmall initial slice

“The Wardbearer is protecting that pack. I can burst the bearer, suppress the ward, or stall the front while another tower gets a clean shot.”

The playable change

Replace selected random waves with authored formations: a protected advance, a fast follow-up behind a slow vanguard, and a staggered attack on two roads. Existing enemy behaviors do the work.

Why come back?

The same map supports different placements, target priorities and support combinations. The challenge is readable before it arrives.

Initial scope

Three formations on two existing maps in an optional encounter trial. Reuse current enemies and effects. One compact threat sentence in the incoming-wave preview.

Cost / failure mode

A formation can become a puzzle with one compulsory answer, or disappear instantly against Crimson. If it only adds health or forces constant target switching, it has failed.

B · Best companion experiment

Optional battlefield contracts

2–3 weeksone objective type

“We can defend the supply cache for two waves and afford the upgrade earlier—or let it go and keep our defense concentrated.”

The playable change

Occasionally offer an extra objective at a pre-authored location. Accepting adds a clearly shown pressure; success earns a modest battle reward and progress toward a cosmetic commemoration.

Why come back?

A familiar map offers a voluntary gamble and a shared co-op story.

Initial scope

One supply-cache contract on two eligible maps, at most once per battle. The player can skip it without penalty. A visible zone has a simple survival condition.

Cost / failure mode

Extra income can snowball into “always accept.” An objective in poor tower coverage can feel unfair. Use authored sites and cap rewards; do not generate arbitrary escort routes.

Mobile & co-op

Offer only between waves. Both active players must agree to add the risk; no answer means skip. One decision, no repeated planting or collection taps. Resume records the accepted objective and its remaining waves.

Stop rule

Rework it if players feel punished for declining, or accept every time without reconsidering placement or spending.

C · More build experimentation

Doctrines for this battle

3–5 weekssmall curated draft

“This run, we’ll turn the road into a chokepoint. Next time we’ll spread our archers across the ridges.”

The playable change

Offer a few run-only tradeoffs at planned breaks: longer-lasting control with less raw damage, or isolated towers trading fire rate for coverage. Each changes how a defense is arranged.

Why come back?

The same unlocked towers can support a different plan each run, without requiring another account grind.

Initial scope

Six authored doctrines, two offers in one optional mode, no stacking duplicates and no paid rerolls. Offer only effects the current map and loadout can actually use.

Cost / failure mode

Balance combinations multiply. Generic attack bonuses become automatic picks, especially on Seraphs. Sidegrades must change a decision and show their drawback plainly.

One shared choice at a break, readable benefit and cost, no modal over a live attack. Disagreement keeps the standard rules. Selections survive reload and co-op-to-solo conversion.

Stop rule

D · Biggest new mode

A short expedition

5–8+ weekshighest uncertainty

“Take the flooded road for a naval advantage, or the ridge road to preserve our supplies before the final siege?”

The playable change

Three short battles with a route choice, limited carryover supplies and a final encounter. Each battlefield ends at a clean save point.

Why come back?

A small campaign produces an arc: preparation, a setback, then a payoff.

Initial scope

One authored route using existing maps, one branch, a deterministic reward, no procedural map generator. Carry a defined supply allowance rather than entire tower placements between maps.

Cost / failure mode

Long sessions, cumulative failure and co-op transitions make this expensive. It must earn its complexity beyond the existing Three Watches, which already returns earlier defenses as echoes.

Distinct from Three Watches

The new decision is which battlefield and resource tradeoff to take next. It should not simply repeat a siege three times with a new name.

Mobile & co-op

Design for approximately 5–8 minutes per leg, then validate the duration. Save after every wave as well as each leg. Rejoining restores the route, inventories and both players’ choices; a disconnected player must not trap the party.

Dependencies

Persistent multi-map session schema, atomic stage transitions, rewards that cannot be duplicated by retrying, a comprehensible route screen and robust solo conversion.

Stop rule

Pause expansion if players mostly quit between legs because the next fight feels like an obligation rather than a choice.

E · A reason to bring victories home

Your Hold, with a purpose

2–4 weeksgoals + challenge links

“That empty plinth is for a battle we’re proud of. Let’s win the next hunt with our support-heavy defense and place it together.”

The playable change

Pin a goal from the existing collection, preview the reward in the courtyard, and launch the relevant battle directly. Add shareable challenge cards with a fixed seed and explicit conditions.

Why come back?

The courtyard tells the story of your play, and gives friends something specific to attempt together.

Initial scope

Three curated collection journeys, one pinned goal, reward previews, and private challenge links. Build on the current Hold editor, catalog and visits.

Cost / failure mode

A prettier checklist will not fix boring combat. Avoid permanent combat bonuses, material farming, expiring streaks or a second daily chore list.

Progress belongs to each account and awards once. Either friend can follow a challenge link; sandbox and incompatible versions are clearly separated from validated records. No public user-content feed in the initial slice.

Stop rule

If players chase rewards but say the battles still feel identical, return to encounter design before adding more collections.

Recommended sequence

Prove the battle is more interesting first.

Start with A. Treat B as a separate follow-up experiment. This isolates what actually makes play better instead of shipping five interacting systems and guessing.

  1. Establish a fair comparison.

    Record representative campaign and +30/+60 Hold the Line situations. Compare equal-gold mixed defenses, a control/support defense and a Seraph-focused defense. Include low and high account progression separately.

  2. Build three encounter templates.

    Protected advance, staggered pursuit, and two-road diversion. Author them on Greenhollow and Frostmere; only offer diversion where two usable roads and affordable coverage exist.

  3. Introduce one clear telegraph.

    Show the enemy’s tactic and entry road before the wave. Keep the first trial at current power budgets; do not combine a new tactic with a hidden health spike.

  4. Test, revise, then decide.

    Use the same seed with different defenses. Keep templates that produce multiple understandable solutions. Only then trial the supply-cache objective separately.

What “more interesting” means

Prototype acceptance: at least two affordable counter approaches per encounter; cues understood without coaching; no need for constant ability tapping; support towers visibly affect the outcome.

Power fantasy: a well-earned Crimson should still overwhelm ordinary waves. Challenge its coverage on suitable maps, rather than making enemies secretly immune to it.

Implementation boundaries and release gates
  • Authoring: add a small encounter data module referenced by the existing wave generator. Store template ID/version, lane choice, seed and wave schedule in deterministic run state. Use no wall-clock randomness or adaptation to the player’s current towers.
  • Compatibility: old saves keep their original generation and balance rules. New encounter sessions pin their content version. Checkpoints and replay journals must reconstruct the same enemies, order and rewards.
  • Co-op: both seats receive identical schedules. Test pause, 1× through 4×, guest rejoin, both directions of solo/co-op conversion, simultaneous choices, retry, defeat and Hold the Line. Compare state hashes after transitions.
  • Difficulty: Casual introduces one pressure at a time; Normal overlaps already learned threats; Veteran tightens timing and coverage. Do not require an expensive late unlock to answer a tactic in early progression.
  • Balance matrix: three defense archetypes × two maps × three difficulties × two late-game depths = 36 core scenarios. Add campaign, low-account and naval-eligible cases separately. This is a planned test matrix, not completed evidence or exhaustive coverage.
  • Readability: real gameplay camera at 390px portrait and 844px landscape, desktop, low quality and reduced motion. Threat cues remain visible with no reliance on sound or subtle color alone.
  • Performance: preserve existing asset-size budgets and enemy caps; reuse current VFX. Compare frame-time and co-op catch-up behavior against the same baseline seed on representative phones.
  • Economy: test reward once-only behavior through retry/reload. New optional modes do not qualify for existing competitive scores or mastery until their conditions are explicitly validated.
  • Release: optional trial first; tune from observed play before adding templates to new endless sessions. Version a rollback so active sessions remain playable.
How we evaluate fun without pretending we have retention data

Begin with six observed testers spanning solo/co-op and newer/experienced players. Use a counterbalanced order: half try the current wave first, half the new encounter. This is qualitative discovery, not a statistically reliable retention study.

Ask what threatened their defense, what they changed, whether another solution seemed possible, and whether they would choose another run. A useful initial target is five of six understanding the cue without explanation. Watch for more varied spending and positioning without more compulsory tapping.

The current project has opt-in battle and wave telemetry. Proposed additions would be encounter exposure, contract acceptance/completion and doctrine choice—only under the same consent. Track quits, performance and return play as guardrails once there is enough real traffic; do not treat more time in a battle as automatically better.

Adversarial design review

Reasons these ideas could fail.

I reviewed the proposal in a separate critical pass, taking the positions of an optimizer, a mobile player and a co-op maintainer. This is a same-assistant review, not an independent reviewer’s sign-off. The revisions below are incorporated above.

R1 · “This just makes everything a Seraph check.”

More enemy health would reinforce the reported endgame dominance.

Revision: equal-gold comparisons first; use timing, coverage and support interactions. Keep Crimson’s payoff. If other families fail economically, correct balance before growing content.

R2 · “You removed a manual Standard button, then added five chores.”

Constant objectives and choices would conflict with the user’s desire for fluid mobile play.

Revision: no new combat action for A; B occurs at most once and between waves; C has only two offers and an unchanged-rules choice.

R3 · “An optional reward is mandatory if it is too efficient.”

Free gold can create a runaway lead or make declining feel wrong.

Revision: contract reward tuning must account for the extra threat and spending. No missed permanent power. Reject the contract if experienced players always accept automatically.

R4 · “My friend disconnected while the decision screen was open.”

New votes and stage transitions can recreate prior stuck-run and split-room bugs.

Revision: choices are recorded run events; default disagreement to unchanged rules; define active voters; rejoin restores committed decisions. Transition and replay tests are release gates.

R5 · “These are features we already have.”

Boss hunts, Mythic powers, linked Watches and the Hold collection already cover some of this territory.

Revision: reuse existing enemy traits; make expeditions about route/resource decisions; make Hold work about guidance and challenge sharing. New boss count alone is not the proposal.

R6 · “You are balancing for a maxed account.”

A counter is not viable if the player has not unlocked it or cannot afford it yet.

Revision: account progression and battle gold are separate test dimensions. Offer low-tier answers. Restrict patterns to appropriate map geometry and progression.

R7 · “Random counters feel like the game is cheating.”

Picking threats in response to a player’s purchases can invalidate their investment.

Revision: generate encounters from fixed seed/map/depth, preview them, and pin the version. Do not inspect the current build to punish it.

R8 · “Everything shipped at once—what actually helped?”

New modes, rewards and tuning would make it hard to diagnose fun or regressions.

Revision: A first, B separately. C–E stay alternatives for discussion. No claim of improved retention until measured.

Unresolved until playtested

How much encounter structure survives extreme late-game power? Will players notice support contributions without another panel? Will optional objectives feel exciting rather than distracting? These uncertainties are why the first slice is intentionally small.
